Atomic Orbitals to Band Gaps

Starting from the intuitions of quantum mechanics and phase overlap, this article aims to build the connection between the modelling of a discrete molecule and that of a periodic crystal.

Classical Mechanics

In classical mechanics we assume a particle can be described with a precise position and momentum. For example

x=x0+v⁢t

And

p=m⁢v

The total energy of a classical particle is defined as the sum of the kinetic and potential energies, called the Hamiltonian, H:

H= p22⁢m +V(r)

Quantum Mechanics

The foundation of quantum mechanics, by contrast, is that we cannot know the position and momentum with infinite precision, and instead they are described by probability distributions. Hence, instead of talking about where a particle is we talk about what is the probability of finding the particle at a given position.

Additionally, we know from observations that particles have wave-like behaviour (the particle undergoes interference like waves combining in a bathtub, see the double slit experiment). This motivates describing the particle by a wave function, a complex value function with an amplitude and phase. The position probability distribution is obtained from the square modulus of the wave function.

p(x)= |ψ(x)| 2

Other properties are extracted by applying operators to the wave function. For example, the momentum operator

p^= −i⁢ℏ⁢∇

And the Hamiltonian operator:

H=− ℏ22⁢m ∇2 +V(r)

A consequence of the properties being entwined in the wave function is that certain properties are correlated, an example being position and momentum, and the more precisely we know one property the less precisely we can know about the other. In terms of the probability distributions, if reduce the width of the distribution of one property, the other must broaden. This is Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

To emphasise, all of this derives from assuming the wave-like nature of particles and behaviour like the uncertainty principle are consequences of the math that captures this perspective. However, it is important to keep in mind this behaviour has been experimentally demonstrated.

Finally, we come to Schrödinger's wave equation. The importance of this equation is that it tells us how to identify valid wave functions of a particle. Simply they must satisfy the equation which states the Hamiltonian operator applied to the wave function must be equal to multiplying the wave function by the energy.

H⁢ψ=E⁢ψ

It can be helpful to think of the wavefunction as a vector and the Hamiltonian being a geometric transformation. The equation is valid for vectors where if apply the transformation the resulting vector is equal to scaling the original vector, making it shorter or longer.

Therefore, to calculate the energy of an atom we just need to use Schrödinger's equation to find the valid wavefunctions of the particles and sum up the energies. Moreover, we typically make the approximation that the nucleus is fixed during the calculation (called the Born-Oppenheimer approximation) and thus we only need to calculate the electronic energy (i.e. the total energy of the electrons).

The Electronic Hamiltonian has four terms: the electron kinetic energy, the electron-nuclei attraction, the electron-electron repulsion, and the nuclei repulsion (a constant)

While the above form of the Schrödinger Equation appears relatively simple, it is far from trivial to solve and is not analytically solvable for systems with more than one electron (due to the many-body problem). Therefore, to solve the wave function the basic approach is to assume that the electrons exist in one electron orbitals (an orbital being a wavefunction only depending on the coordinates of one electron) and are non-interacting. The electron-electron interaction is partially included in an average way through a 'mean-field' that avoids the many-body problem, however, this is still an approximation.

So with this approach we can solve the atomic energy but how about for a molecule? For this we take the approach that the molecular orbitals are formed by linearly combining atomic orbitals from the atoms. The atomic orbitals are then a 'basis set' for approximating the molecular orbitals. In theory, we could use a basis set of any functions, however, we know the atomic orbitals already have features expected for electron orbitals, and so can give good results with relatively few atomic orbital functions.

Generally we are interested in the lowest energy solution for the molecular orbitals, called the ground state and for this we can apply the variational theorem to determine the coefficients. This theorem is that if we alter the coefficients of the combination and the energy is lower, then this solution is closer to the ground state. We can keep altering the coefficients until we can't lower the energy further at which point we are closest to the ground state (with the basis set).

One aspect we need to consider when combining orbitals is the wave-like nature and if the orbitals combine in phase, leading to constructive overlap (amplitudes add), or if combine out-of-phase, leading to destructive overlap (amplitudes subtract). The in-phase case leads to the electron delocalising across both atoms represented by the position probability being positive over the atoms, whereas the out-of-phase case leads to a 'node' (i.e. zero probability point).

Remembering that the position and momentum are related by the uncertainty principle, electrons being more localised to a specific region means their momentum must increase, leading to higher kinetic energy. Therefore, nodes lead to higher energy states.

This idea goes all the way to the solid state where we have essentially infinite combinations of orbitals. However, rather than talking about individual combinations, we instead have continuous energy 'bands' which are parameterised by a k-point that indexes the combination. The energy thus becomes a function of this k-point. Fortunately, the energy typically varies slowly with the k-point value (for organic materials) and hence we can get accurate energies by evaluating a small number of k-points. The commonly reported 'band gap' is the energy between the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied bands when we fill the bands with the available electrons (from the atomic orbitals).

Note that this is referring to the periodicity of the electronic structure. A related concept is the motion of atoms.

We know that atoms cannot be at rest and so must be in constant motion, this is the zero-point energy. These motions can, similar to the electronic orbitals, be in-phase or out-of-phase with neighbouring atoms. Therefore, applying the same idea we end up with phonon bands (also called dispersion), which describe the energy of the atomic motions in solids and is also parameterised by a k-value, though it is typically denoted q. Moreover, vibrations that constrain the atoms tighter, have higher zero-point energy by the same reasoning above
